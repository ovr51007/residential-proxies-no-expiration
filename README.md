## The three clocks hiding behind "no expiration"

When a proxy provider says nothing expires, they usually mean the balance. When you ask support why your setup stopped working, the answer is often about one of the other two.

| Clock | What it actually measures | How it burns you | Question to ask before paying |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Balance / traffic expiry | How long purchased GB or unactivated IPs sit in your account | Unused traffic vanishes on a date you did not track | "If I use 10% this month, is the rest still there in four months?" |
| Proxy lifespan | How long a single activated IP stays online | Your session drops mid-task because the IP rotated or went offline | "How long does one IP stay alive, and can I reuse it?" |
| Billing cycle reset | Whether the plan renews and resets monthly | Paying for a full month to run a three-day job | "Is this a subscription or a one-off top-up?" |

Most "no expiration" complaints turn out to be clock two or clock three. A provider with non-expiring traffic that still forces a monthly subscription has not actually solved anything for someone running project-based work.

## What happens after you activate an IP

This is clock two, and it deserves a straight answer rather than marketing language.

Each activated proxy stays online for anywhere from a few hours to about 24 hours. That range is normal for rotating residential networks — these are real devices on real home connections, and devices go offline. What matters more is what happens next: if a proxy drops and then comes back online, port forwarding lets you reuse it free through the "Today List" in the client, within a 24-hour window.

So the accurate version of "9Proxy IPs never expire" is: *unactivated IPs never expire, and activated IPs can be re-pulled for free inside a 24-hour reuse window.* That is a genuinely different product from a static ISP proxy, where you rent one IP for a month. If what you actually need is one permanent, never-changing IP address, this is not that, and no amount of "no expiration" marketing changes it.

The company has also published a 60-second refund rule: an IP that fails to connect within its first minute of use gets credited back. Worth knowing, since a large IP pack that returns dead addresses is exactly where per-IP billing usually goes wrong.

## Which model you should actually pick

Once you separate non-expiring IPs from the 180-day traffic window, the choice becomes arithmetic instead of vibes.

**Bandwidth-heavy, IP-light work** — large crawl jobs, media downloads, anything where a handful of IPs push hundreds of gigabytes. The IP-based tiers win here, and it isn't close: 5,000 IPs cost $360 with unlimited bandwidth on each. To buy the same volume of traffic on the GB side, you would be looking at several hundred dollars' worth of packs, with the clock running from the day you buy.

**IP-heavy, bandwidth-light work** — multi-account management, ad verification, checking how a page renders in twelve cities. GB-based packs are usually cheaper here, because each request moves kilobytes and you are paying for breadth of IPs rather than volume of data.

**Intermittent project work** — agency work that arrives in bursts. The IP-based side is the natural fit precisely because of the no-expiration rule: buy 500 IPs during a busy month, leave the leftovers sitting for the next client, and lose nothing. That is the scenario the search phrase is really about.

One caution on the GB side: the effective rate falls fast with volume, but every pack carries the same 180-day validity. Buying 2,000 GB to get $0.75 per GB only makes sense if you have a realistic path to burning 2 TB in six months. Otherwise you are paying upfront for a discount you will never consume — the exact problem non-expiring IPs were meant to solve. Only the enterprise tiers remove that constraint.
